Chapter IV

The Disappearance of Ribbons

In the morning, the disappearance was quiet. It was ribbons. Delicate emerald and vermilion ribbons fluttered from open windows toward the river, carried away into the cold dawn air without a whisper or a protest. They glided between telegraph poles and across cedar gables like unspooling memories, dissolving into the mist.

I had stepped out onto the balcony while the kettle still whistled faintly upon the cast-iron stove. My neighbor, an elderly milliner whose fingers were permanently dyed indigo from thread, leaned against her wooden railing with empty palms upturned to the grey sky. mode_comment 1 note • 32 reader highlights She smiled faintly, not with sorrow, but with the hollow tranquility of someone who has dropped an old coin into very deep water.

“Do you remember what they were for?” she asked as I passed her gate an hour later. Her voice held neither alarm nor curiosity, merely the rhythmic calm of seasonal rain.

I paused, searching the small wooden chest inside my mind where names, uses, and textures were usually filed. But the compartment was already vacant, scrubbed smooth like driftwood stranded on gravel. “No,” I confessed, wrapping my woolen shawl tighter against the chill. “I only recall they were soft.”

By noon, the Island was spotless. Children were playing with pebbles near the dry well, and neither of them looked up when a solitary strip of silk caught in the high boughs of the camphor tree and dissolved into ash under the pale winter sun.
